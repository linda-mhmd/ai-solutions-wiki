NVIDIA announced on 12 November 2026 that **Jensen Huang**, who co-founded the company in 1993 and ran it for 33 years, has resigned as president, chief executive officer and member of the board, **effective immediately**. **Colette Kress**, executive vice president and chief financial officer since 2013, is **interim CEO** and keeps the CFO role. Director **Harvey C. Jones**, a former CEO of Synopsys, becomes NVIDIA's **first-ever chair of the board**. The board is searching for a permanent CEO and says it will consider internal and external candidates.

This entry separates what NVIDIA has stated, what has been reported, and what can be measured, because the week produced a great deal of all three.

## What NVIDIA has said

From NVIDIA's press release of 12 November and its Form 8-K filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission:

- Huang resigned from all three roles with immediate effect. The 8-K gives **11 November 2026** as the date of the earliest event reported, which matches reporting that the board accepted the resignation on Wednesday evening; the announcement came the next morning.
- Kress, 59, serves as interim CEO while the board runs the search.
- Jones becomes chair. NVIDIA has not had a separate chair before.

NVIDIA's announcement does **not** give a reason for the resignation and, as reported, does not say what has happened to the board's independent investigation into Huang. NVIDIA is still scheduled to report fiscal third-quarter 2027 results on **17 November 2026**, Kress's first test in the role.

## What led up to it

These are reported facts, not findings, and they are attributed accordingly.

- **The New York Times** reported that policymakers in the Trump administration had received an intelligence memo in the spring identifying a Singapore-based technology executive, with whom Huang had a relationship, as having been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. China's government called the "so-called Chinese spy narrative" "entirely baseless". No charges have been reported.
- Huang **acknowledged the relationship** in a memo to staff, according to multiple outlets. NVIDIA's board then announced an **independent investigation** by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel, saying it had no reason to believe the company's secrets or US national security had been compromised.
- Members of Congress from both parties called for scrutiny of the matter.
- Midweek, further reports described **a second relationship, with a subordinate NVIDIA executive**. The Wall Street Journal then reported that the board was preparing to remove Huang, which Reuters said it had confirmed independently. The board meeting on 11 November ran into the evening, and the resignation was announced on the morning of the 12th.

We are deliberately not naming the two individuals, neither of whom holds public office, and the allegation of espionage remains an allegation.

## What it did to the stock

The market reaction is the measurable part. The figures below are reported intraday numbers from 12 November, while trading was still under way; we have not been able to confirm a closing price at the time of writing, and different outlets measured at different moments.

| When | What was reported |
| --- | --- |
| Mon 9 Nov | Shares closed down about 4.3% after Huang acknowledged the relationship (CNBC) |
| Mon 9 to Tue 10 Nov | Down about 9.5% across the two sessions |
| Wed 11 Nov | Closed at $173.73; then fell about 8% after hours on the second report and the board report |
| Thu 12 Nov, pre-market | Down about 11% to 12.5%, quoted around $152 to $155 |
| Thu 12 Nov, after the open | Down as much as 13% to 14%, a reported low near $149, erasing close to $600 billion of market value at the trough, before paring to a loss of roughly 7% to 10%. Market value fell below $4 trillion |
| Week to date | CBS reported NVIDIA on track to lose more than $700 billion of value since the previous week's close |

**The wider market, as reported during the session:** the Nasdaq Composite was down about 1.5% and the S&P 500 about 0.7%, while the Dow, which weights NVIDIA less heavily, was up. The iShares Semiconductor ETF fell about 3.2%. AMD and Broadcom, by contrast, held up better, with traders betting that NVIDIA's troubles could send business their way.

**Analysts, as reported:** Bernstein halved its price target from $400 to $200, and was reported to have downgraded the stock to market-perform; Stifel cut its target from $315 to $205 but kept its buy rating; Citi cut from $285 to $225. FactSet counted at least 10 analysts turning more bearish during the week. JPMorgan said the risk of tighter restrictions on chip shipments into China had risen, because Huang had been NVIDIA's chief advocate in Washington for easing them.

## Reading the numbers carefully

A few points of method, because a headline percentage is not a measurement of what Huang was worth.

**This is not a clean event.** Event studies in finance measure an *abnormal* return: the stock's move minus what the market model predicts it would have done anyway. Here the "event" was spread over four days of leaks, admissions and reports, so no single-day move isolates the resignation. Much of the decline came *before* the announcement, as the market priced in a rising probability that Huang would go.

**The benchmark is contaminated.** The usual correction is to subtract the market's move. But NVIDIA is one of the largest weights in the Nasdaq and the S&P 500, so on 12 November the index fell partly *because* NVIDIA fell. Subtracting the Nasdaq's 1.5% from NVIDIA's drop understates the company-specific effect. A careful estimate would use a benchmark that excludes NVIDIA, which we have not computed.

**Dollar losses depend on the moment.** "Nearly $600 billion" was measured at the intraday low; "$300 billion to $440 billion" figures were measured later in the session. All are snapshots of a price that kept moving, and none is a realised loss.

**What the research says.** Three strands of academic work help put the move in context:

- **Sudden departures reveal how much the market valued an executive.** Johnson, Magee, Nagarajan and Newman (1985) studied the stock-price reaction to 53 sudden executive deaths and found that the reaction varied with whether the executive was a founder and with their decision-making responsibilities. Nguyen and Nielsen (2014) extended the method to 149 sudden deaths of US executives and found that the market's reaction was *positive* in 42% of CEO cases, and that top executives capture on average about 71% of the value they create (65% for CEOs). In this literature, founder-CEOs tend to carry the largest valuation premium, which is consistent with the size of NVIDIA's move. A resignation is not a death, but the logic is the same: an unexpected exit shows what the market thought one person was worth.
- **Personal scandals cost firms money, beyond the individual.** Cline, Walkling and Yore (2018, *Journal of Financial Economics*), studying roughly 35 years of managerial indiscretions, found an average firm-value loss of about 4.1% when the indiscretion involved the CEO and 1.6% for other managers, with continued underperformance and lost partnerships and customers afterwards. NVIDIA's move this week was several times that average, which is what you would expect if the market was pricing the loss of the founder and the China-policy risk rather than the scandal alone.
- **Boards act faster on personal misconduct than on financial misconduct.** Research by Aaron Hill and colleagues at the University of Florida, comparing 59 personal-misconduct cases with more than 300 financial scandals from 1997 to 2020, found CEOs were about five times more likely to be dismissed for personal misconduct than for overseeing financial fraud. NVIDIA's board moved within four days of the first report.

The honest conclusion is that the market treated Huang as a large part of NVIDIA's value, as founder-CEO research would predict, and that the week's moves bundle together the loss of the founder, uncertainty about succession, the investigation, and the policy risk around China. Separating those requires the earnings call on 17 November and a proper event study after the dust settles.

Away from the trading floor, the whole internet reacted with memes, riffing on the Coldplay "kiss cam" clip, Austin Powers and Huang's teenage job at Denny's, and one of the most shared images, a studded version of his trademark leather jacket, turned out to be AI-generated.

## What it means for builders

- **Nothing you have bought changes today.** GPUs, DGX systems, CUDA, NIM and NeMo, and existing supply and cloud contracts are unaffected by a change of CEO. Roadmaps such as Vera Rubin were set long before this week. See [NVIDIA AI platform](/tools/nvidia-ai/).
- **Watch three dates and decisions.** The 17 November results and guidance; who is named permanent CEO, and whether it is an insider who keeps the roadmap or an outsider who changes priorities; and any shift in US export policy towards China, where NVIDIA has lost its most effective advocate.
- **Treat it as a vendor-concentration reminder.** Most AI infrastructure still runs on one vendor's hardware and software stack. A leadership crisis at that vendor is a good prompt to check how hard it would be for you to run on other accelerators, or to buy compute through a cloud provider that can switch underneath you. The [AI compute build-out](/news/ai-compute-buildout-2026/) and [bubble concerns](/news/ai-infrastructure-bubble-concerns-2026/) entries cover the wider exposure.
